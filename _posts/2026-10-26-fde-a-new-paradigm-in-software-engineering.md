#### <span class="tc-teal">What the role is:</span>

A Forward Deployed Engineer (FDE) is a software engineer who works directly with a customer, often on-site or embedded in their teams, to make a product solve that customer’s real problem. A regular product engineer builds features for many customers from inside the company. An FDE starts from one customer’s messy situation and builds whatever is needed to make the product deliver value there.

Palantir made the role well known in the 2010s. Its software was powerful but needed heavy adaptation to each client’s data and workflows, so it sent engineers into government agencies and enterprises to do that work. The role has since spread widely, especially among AI companies such as OpenAI, Anthropic and many startups. Their models are general-purpose, and value only appears once someone connects them to a company’s data, systems and processes.

#### <span class="tc-teal">What an FDE actually does:</span>

- <strong>Understands the business problem.</strong> Sits with users, learns how work really gets done, and finds where the product can make a measurable difference.
- <strong>Writes production code.</strong> Builds data pipelines, integrations, custom workflows, agents and evaluation setups on top of the core platform. This is real engineering, not configuration or demos.
- <strong>Deals with the client’s environment.</strong> Works within their security rules, legacy systems, data quality issues and approval processes.
- <strong>Feeds learning back to the product team.</strong> When the same workaround is built for three customers, the FDE pushes for it to become a product feature. This loop is a large part of the role’s value to the vendor.

#### <span class="tc-teal">Skills the role needs:</span>

1. Strong engineering: Python or similar, APIs, data engineering, cloud platforms, and now LLM application patterns (retrieval, agents, evaluation).
2. Problem framing: turning a vague ask (“use AI in claims processing”) into a specific, buildable use case with a success metric.
3. Client communication: explaining trade-offs to both engineers and executives, and handling pushback.
4. Comfort with ambiguity: requirements change, access gets delayed, data is worse than expected.
5. Judgment about what to generalize: knowing when a custom fix should stay custom and when it should go back to the product.
